If you are hunting for **payday loans that accept KOHO**, the short answer is that plenty of Canadian lenders will work with your account — but approval does not hinge on one yes-or-no question. It hinges on three separate systems, and a KOHO account passes them at three different rates. This guide walks through each one, explains the naming quirk that causes most false rejections, and gives you seven checks to run before you apply.

## Quick Answer

Yes — **payday loans that accept KOHO** exist, and the account clears the two mechanical hurdles easily: it can receive an Interac e-Transfer, and it can accept a pre-authorized debit for repayment. The hurdle that actually trips people up is verification, because KOHO does not hold your money under its own name. Find the right institution in the lender's menu and most applications proceed normally.

## The Three Systems Behind Payday Loans That Accept KOHO

"Does this lender accept KOHO?" is really three questions wearing one coat. Lenders run three independent systems, and they can fail separately:

| System | What it does | How a KOHO account performs |
|---|---|---|
| **Verification** | Confirms your income and account history | Usually fine — supported by major connection providers, but listed under a partner institution's name |
| **Funding** | Sends the money to you | Strong — Interac e-Transfer runs on your email address, not your account number |
| **Repayment** | Pulls the payment on the due date | Works, but there is no overdraft cushion if the balance is short |

Most articles on this topic answer only the first question. The other two decide whether the loan actually works out for you, so read on.

## Why Your Account Shows as Peoples Trust, Not KOHO

Here is the detail that causes more failed applications than anything else. KOHO is a financial technology company, not a chartered bank. It partners with **Peoples Trust Company** to hold customer funds, which means the direct-deposit and pre-authorized debit details in your KOHO app carry that institution's routing information — institution number **621** — rather than a KOHO-branded one.

The practical consequence: when a lender's bank-selection dropdown has no "KOHO" entry, you are usually looking for the wrong name. Search the menu for Peoples Trust. If the lender asks you to type an institution and transit number manually, take them from the direct-deposit screen in your KOHO app rather than guessing.

This is also why some borrowers conclude a lender "declined KOHO" when nothing of the sort happened — the application simply never got past a search box.

## KOHO's Status Is Changing (and That Matters)

KOHO announced in June 2026 that it had raised $130 million, valuing the company at roughly $1.33 billion, and described itself as being in the final stages of a multi-year Schedule I bank licence application with the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions. A licence would still require ministerial sign-off, so nothing is settled.

Why should a borrower care? Because the partner-institution arrangement above is a consequence of KOHO not yet holding its own licence. If and when that changes, KOHO would appear in bank menus under its own name and the most common cause of failed applications would quietly disappear. Until then, treat the naming quirk as the normal state of affairs, not a glitch.

## Verification: What Connects and What Doesn't

Verification is where most applications for payday loans that accept KOHO actually stall. Canadian short-term lenders overwhelmingly verify income by connecting to your account rather than reading pay stubs. Both **Flinks** and **Plaid** support KOHO connections in Canada, and Flinks is the more commonly used of the two among domestic lenders. Inverite is a third Canadian option some lenders run.

If a connection attempt fails, the cause is usually one of these, and most are fixable in a minute:

- **A two-factor prompt timed out.** Keep your phone in hand and complete the code request promptly.
- **You entered a card number where a username was expected.** Check which credential the login screen actually wants.
- **Your account history is too short.** A brand-new account with three weeks of activity gives an underwriter almost nothing to read.
- **The lender's provider is having an outage.** Try again later before assuming a decline.

If the connection genuinely will not work, ask whether the lender accepts PDF statements. Manual review is slower and happens in business hours, but it is a real alternative.

## Funding: Where the Money Actually Lands

Funding is the gate people worry about most and the one that fails least, because of how the rail works.

An Interac e-Transfer is addressed to your **email address or mobile number**, not to an account number. The receiving institution is resolved after you accept the transfer, when you choose where to deposit it — or automatically, if you have autodeposit enabled. That is why a lender can fund an account it has never heard of: it never needed to know which institution held your money.

Two practical points follow. First, **turn autodeposit on before you apply.** Without it, a transfer waits on a security question, and a mistyped answer or an unread email can leave funds sitting for hours after the lender considers the loan advanced. Second, the deposit notification is your proof of the advance date — keep it, because your repayment date and any cancellation window count from there.

One change on the horizon is worth knowing about. Payments Canada's Real-Time Rail is scheduled to launch in late 2026, with its by-law and rules in force since 24 August 2026. Until then, "instant" in Canadian lending means Interac e-Transfer running on batch settlement behind the scenes. It usually clears in minutes, but it is not the same thing as true real-time settlement, and evenings, weekends and holidays can still introduce delay.

## Repayment: The Part Nobody Plans For

Funding is the easy half. Repayment is where a KOHO account behaves differently from a chequing account at a big bank, and it is worth understanding before you sign.

Traditional banks often sell overdraft protection, which advances you the shortfall for a fee when a payment lands and the balance is low. KOHO does not offer a bank overdraft in that form. So if your pre-authorized debit hits and the money is not there, the payment is **returned**, not covered.

That triggers two charges, and the arithmetic has recently flipped:

| Charge | Who charges it | Current limit |
|---|---|---|
| NSF / returned-item fee | Your financial institution | **$10** at federally regulated institutions since 12 March 2026 |
| Dishonoured-payment fee | The payday lender | **$20** maximum |
| Default interest | The payday lender | **2.5% per month**, non-compounding |

For years the bank's NSF fee was the painful half at roughly $45 to $48. It is now capped at $10 for federally regulated institutions — which means the lender's $20 fee has become the larger charge. Any article still telling you the bank fee is the main risk is working from pre-2026 numbers.

Note too that the dishonoured-payment fee and default interest sit **outside** the federal cap on payday borrowing costs. That cap limits the cost of borrowing to $14 per $100 advanced; it does not limit what a failed payment costs you.

## Seven Checks Before You Apply for Payday Loans That Accept KOHO

1. **Open your KOHO direct-deposit screen** and copy the institution, transit and account numbers exactly.
2. **Search the lender's bank menu for the partner institution**, not for "KOHO".
3. **Confirm the lender is licensed** in your province before you share anything. Provincial registries are public and free to search.
4. **Check that you have 60–90 days of deposit history** in the account you are connecting.
5. **Ask which connection provider the lender uses** — if one fails repeatedly, another lender may use a different one.
6. **Line up the due date with your actual pay date**, not the day after, so a delayed deposit does not cause a return.
7. **Price the whole thing in dollars**, not percentages: $14 per $100 on a two-week loan is a very high annualised rate.

## What This Costs — and the Cheaper Comparison

Under the federal Criminal Interest Rate Regulations in force since 1 January 2025, a payday loan is exempt from the 35% annual criminal interest rate only if it is under $1,500, runs 62 days or less, comes from a licensed lender in a designated province, and costs no more than **14% of the amount advanced**. That is the ceiling in every designated province — older provincial figures of $15 or $17 per $100 are out of date.

That ceiling applies to payday loans that accept KOHO exactly as it applies to any other licensed payday lender in a designated province — the account you hold changes nothing about the price. Before borrowing, compare against a regulated [installment loan](/resources/guides/payday-vs-installment-loans), which spreads repayment over months under that 35% ceiling instead of demanding a lump sum on your next payday. For many borrowers with a KOHO account, that is the cheaper path — and it uses exactly the same connection and deposit rails.

## The Bottom Line

Searching for **payday loans that accept KOHO** is the right instinct, but the better question is which of the three systems your application will stumble on. Verification is a naming problem with a simple fix. Funding is rarely an issue. Repayment is the one that costs money, because there is no overdraft cushion behind a short balance — and the lender's $20 dishonoured-payment fee now outweighs the $10 your institution can charge. Get those three right and the account itself is rarely the obstacle.
