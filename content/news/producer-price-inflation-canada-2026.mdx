## Brief summary

**Producer price inflation Canada** measured in July came in at **12.4%** year over year. Consumer inflation, reported three days earlier, came in at **3.0%**. That is a four-fold gap between what factories charge and what households pay — and it is far less alarming than it first appears.

![Producer price inflation Canada records at the factory gate rarely matches what shoppers pay in stores and malls](https://images.pexels.com/photos/374894/pexels-photo-374894.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## Introduction

Statistics Canada published the July Industrial Product Price Index on **August 20, 2026**, three days after the July Consumer Price Index. Set side by side, the two readings look irreconcilable:

| Index | July 2026, year over year |
|---|---|
| Industrial Product Price Index (IPPI) | **+12.4%** |
| Raw Materials Price Index (RMPI) | **+18.1%** |
| Consumer Price Index (CPI) | **+3.0%** |

The instinctive reading — that a wave of cost inflation is about to hit consumers — is not what the data supports. Understanding why is genuinely useful for anyone trying to read Canadian inflation.

## The main story: 3 reasons the producer price inflation Canada gap exists

**1. Different baskets.** The IPPI is weighted toward energy, metals and industrial commodities. The CPI is weighted toward what households actually spend on — and in July that included **shelter up just 1.3%** and services that barely moved. Roughly speaking, the producer index measures things Canada digs up and refines; the consumer index measures where Canadians live and what they do.

**2. Different buyers.** A large share of what the IPPI covers is **exported**, not sold to Canadians. Unwrought silver up **60.3%** year over year and plastic resins up **46.3%** are world-market prices. They inflate the Canadian producer index without ever appearing in a Canadian household budget.

**3. Currency.** Many of these commodities are priced in **US dollars**. When the loonie weakens, the identical physical commodity records a higher Canadian-dollar price. That lifts the producer indexes mechanically. The consumer index is far less exposed to this effect.

## Background: July composition undercuts the producer price inflation Canada headline

Look inside the monthly numbers and the pipeline story weakens further.

- IPPI: **+0.6%** month over month
- IPPI **excluding energy and petroleum products: −0.2%**
- RMPI: **−2.2%** month over month
- Primary non-ferrous metals: **−5.2%**; aluminum alloys **−7.0%**, the largest fall since April 2025
- Chemicals: **−3.4%**; fertilizers and pesticides **−10.8%**
- Metal ores and concentrates: **−4.7%**

So in the month itself, what producers **paid** for raw materials fell 2.2%, and what they **charged** rose only because of refined fuel. Outside energy, factory-gate prices declined.

A genuine cost-push wave would show broad increases across categories. This shows one.

## Why producer price inflation Canada watchers should read composition first

The pattern repeats across Canadian data this month. July's consumer inflation headline rose to 3.0% while core measures sat near **2.0%** and CPI excluding gasoline printed **2.2%** for a third straight month. In both the consumer and producer releases, a single energy component moved the headline while the underlying basket was calmer.

For households and borrowers, three practical points follow:

- **Do not annualise a headline you have not decomposed.** A 12.4% producer figure and a 3.0% consumer figure describe different economies.
- **Watch the ex-energy series.** In July it was negative on the producer side and 2.2% on the consumer side. That is the cleaner signal.
- **Fuel is the transmission channel that does reach you.** Diesel rose **10.4%** and gasoline **4.7%** at the factory gate in July, which affects transport and freight costs regardless of what the rest of the index did.

![Producer price inflation Canada households feel arrives through fuel and freight rather than through the headline index](https://images.pexels.com/photos/8292883/pexels-photo-8292883.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1200)

## How to read the two indexes side by side

The practical skill here is knowing which series answers which question. They are not competing estimates of the same thing.

| Question | Index to use | July 2026 reading |
|---|---|---|
| What is happening to my cost of living? | **CPI** | **+3.0%** y/y |
| Is that broad or driven by one item? | **CPI ex-gasoline** | **+2.2%** y/y |
| What is the Bank of Canada targeting? | **CPI-median / CPI-trim** | **~2.0%** |
| What are factories charging? | **IPPI** | **+12.4%** y/y |
| Is that broad or driven by one item? | **IPPI ex-energy** | **−0.2%** m/m |
| What are producers paying for inputs? | **RMPI** | **−2.2%** m/m |

Every index has an "excluding" variant for a reason, and in July every one of those variants told a calmer story than its headline. That is the single most reliable habit for reading Canadian price data: find the ex-energy series before forming a view.

It also explains why **producer price inflation Canada** publishes rarely makes the evening news while CPI always does. One measures a household's bills; the other measures a supply chain that mostly serves other businesses and export markets.

## Key takeaways

- **Producer price inflation Canada** posted was **12.4%** year over year in July 2026, against **3.0%** consumer inflation.
- The gap reflects different baskets, export-weighted commodities and US-dollar pricing — not imminent consumer inflation.
- Excluding energy, the producer index **fell 0.2%** on the month.
- The Raw Materials Price Index **fell 2.2%**, so input costs eased.
- Metals, chemicals and fertilizers all declined; **aluminum alloys fell 7.0%**.
- The IPPI has now risen year over year for **22 consecutive months**.

## Conclusion

The four-fold gap between **producer price inflation Canada** recorded and the consumer figure is a measurement artifact far more than a warning. Different baskets, different buyers and a currency effect explain most of it, and the monthly composition — falling raw materials, falling metals, falling fertilizer — argues against a cost-push wave.

What survives the decomposition is fuel. That is the one channel in this release with a clear path to household budgets, and it is worth tracking on its own rather than through a headline that averages it with silver and plastic resins.
