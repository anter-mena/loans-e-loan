Canada's proposed **consumer-driven banking regulations** are open for public comment until **August 26, 2026**, and they are the most consequential change to how Canadians share their financial data in a generation. Pre-published in the Canada Gazette, Part I on **June 27, 2026**, they implement the Consumer-Driven Banking Act and create a **Bank of Canada–supervised** framework for secure, API-based sharing of consumer and business financial information.

*Reported August 2, 2026, based on the Canada Gazette, Part I publication of June 27, 2026 and Department of Finance materials. These are proposed regulations; final versions must still be published. This is news reporting and general information, not financial or legal advice.*

## What the consumer-driven banking regulations actually change

The practical answer is: how a third-party app gets at your banking data, and who is accountable when something goes wrong.

**Today**, a large share of Canadian budgeting apps, lenders and account-aggregation tools reach your data one of two ways — through a commercial data provider such as Flinks, Plaid or Inverite, or, at the smaller end of the market, by asking you to hand over your online banking username and password so the service can log in and read the screen. That second practice, screen scraping, sits in a grey zone: it often breaches your bank's terms, and the liability position if funds go missing is murky.

**Under the framework**, participants must be **accredited**, connect through defined **APIs**, and operate under published technical standards, service levels and liability rules. Consent becomes explicit and, importantly, revocable.

That last point deserves emphasis. Under today's arrangements, withdrawing a service's access to your account is frequently unclear and sometimes impossible without changing your banking password. The consumer-driven banking regulations set out participant duties around consent that are designed to make revocation a defined right rather than a customer-service request.

## Background: how Canada arrived at the consumer-driven banking regulations

Canada has been working toward this framework for years, well behind the United Kingdom, Australia and the European Union. The Consumer-Driven Banking Act established the statutory basis; the consumer-driven banking regulations put the operating detail underneath it.

The June 27 publication was accompanied by a separate set of proposed amendments to the Financial Consumer Protection Framework Regulations aimed at consumer-targeted fraud — a different instrument, with its own timeline, that would require banks to obtain express consent before activating certain electronic funds transfer capabilities on personal deposit accounts.

The framework is also expected to interact with Canada's **Real-Time Rail**, the new payments infrastructure anticipated to launch in the fourth quarter of 2026. Data sharing plus real-time payments is the combination that makes account switching and pay-by-bank realistic, which is why the write-access phase matters more than the read-access phase for day-to-day banking.

## Why this matters for borrowers

This is not an abstract policy story for anyone who has applied for credit outside a big bank.

Most Canadian alternative lenders already decide applications by scanning roughly **90 days of bank transactions** — deposits, overdraft days, NSF returns, existing loan payments — rather than relying on a credit score alone. That is why a borrower with a damaged bureau file but steady deposits is often approved, and a borrower with a good score but chaotic cash flow is often declined.

The framework affects that in three ways:

- **Cash-flow underwriting becomes mainstream.** Standardised, accredited access should extend to lenders that currently will not touch screen-scraped data. For borrowers whose bank account looks better than their credit report, that is a meaningful widening of options.
- **Consent becomes a right you can exercise.** Revoking access after an application is currently inconsistent. Defined participant duties change that.
- **Password handover should end.** Until the framework operates, one safe habit stands: prefer lenders that connect through a named data provider over any lender asking you to type your banking password into their own form.

Two cautions worth stating plainly. First, the consumer-driven banking regulations are **proposals** — scope and detail can change before final publication. Second, the Bank of Canada indicated earlier in 2026 that it would be premature to fix a firm operational date until the required work is fully understood, so timelines should be treated as direction rather than schedule.

## Key takeaways

- The **consumer-driven banking regulations** were pre-published **June 27, 2026** and are open for comment until **August 26, 2026**.
- The **Bank of Canada** supervises the framework.
- Phase one covers **read access** to data; payments and account switching come later.
- Accreditation, defined liability and revocable consent replace today's screen-scraping practice.
- For borrowers, the practical effect is broader, more standardised **cash-flow underwriting**.
- They remain **proposals** — final regulations must still be published.

## What to watch next

Watch for the final regulations in the Canada Gazette, Part II after the comment period closes, and for the Bank of Canada's accreditation criteria — the list of who qualifies as a participant will determine how quickly consumers see anything change.
